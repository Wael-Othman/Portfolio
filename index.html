Correlation Matrix — true diversification
💡 How to use this — and what it tells you ▶
What it does
Measures how your holdings move relative to each other, using about a year of daily price returns. Diversification isn't about owning many stocks — it's about owning stocks that don't all fall on the same day. This shows whether you actually have that.
How to use it
  1. Make sure your free FMP key is set (Valuation → DCF → FMP Key).
  2. Click Build Matrix. It fetches ~1 year of prices per stock/ETF holding (one lookup each) and confirms before spending quota.
  3. Read across any row to see how that holding relates to every other.
Reading the colors
  • +1.0 (deep red) = move in lockstep — owning both adds little diversification.
  • 0 (pale) = unrelated — good, they cushion each other.
  • −1.0 (deep green) = move oppositely — a natural hedge.
  • The diagonal is always 1.00 (each stock vs. itself).
What to look for
  • Lots of red? Your portfolio is more concentrated than the number of tickers suggests — a single shock could hit many positions at once.
  • Clusters of red usually map to a sector (e.g. your tech names). Genuine diversification means greens and pales across clusters.
Good to know
  • Run it occasionally, not every visit — it uses one FMP lookup per holding.
  • Free daily history is US-strongest; foreign tickers without data are dropped and listed below the matrix.
  • Correlations shift over time (especially in a crash, when many things converge toward +1) — treat it as a recent snapshot, not a constant.
Green = low / negative correlation (diversifying); red = moves together (concentrated). One FMP lookup per holding.

💡 How to use this — and what a DCF actually tells you ▶
The 30-second version
  1. Click FMP Key once, paste a free key (financialmodelingprep.com → sign up → copy key).
  2. Type a ticker or pick one of your holdings, hit Fetch & Build.
  3. The numbers fill in. Adjust the growth and discount rate assumptions to match your own view.
  4. Read the Intrinsic Value / Share and how it compares to today's price. Use the sensitivity grid to see how fragile that number is.
What it's doing
A DCF estimates what a business is worth today by projecting its future free cash flow and discounting it back to present value. This one uses a two-stage model: a high-growth period (Stage 1), a fade period (Stage 2), then a perpetual terminal growth rate. It adds cash, subtracts debt, and divides by shares to get value per share.
The inputs that matter most
Base FCFLatest annual free cash flow. The starting point everything grows from.
Stage 1 GrowthHow fast FCF grows in the near term. Auto-derived from history — sanity-check it.
Terminal GrowthForever-growth after the forecast. Keep it low — 2–3% (≈ long-run GDP). Never above your discount rate.
Discount Rate (WACC)Your required return / risk. Higher = more cautious = lower value. 8–10% is typical for large caps.
Reading the result
  • Upside / (Down) = how far today's price sits below (or above) your fair value. A positive number means the model sees it as cheap.
  • Roughly ±20% is treated as "fair" — DCFs aren't precise enough to act on small gaps.
  • The sensitivity matrix shifts WACC and terminal growth a notch each way. If fair value swings wildly across the grid, the model is fragile — lean on your assumptions, not the headline.
Good to know
  • DCF fits cash-generative, predictable businesses best. It's a poor fit for banks, early-stage / unprofitable companies, and most crypto.
  • Free data is strongest for US tickers. Foreign listings (e.g. VOW3.DE, 9618.HK) may return partial data — fill the amber gaps by hand.
  • Free FMP allows 250 lookups/day — far more than you'll need.
  • This is a thinking tool, not advice. Garbage in, garbage out — always pressure-test the growth rate.

↺ Reverse DCF — what is the price implying?
Holds today's price as fair value and solves for the Stage-1 FCF growth the market is baking in — using your current WACC, terminal growth, Stage-1 years, debt, cash and shares. Compare it to the growth a company can realistically deliver. How to read it ▾ The idea: a normal DCF asks "given growth X, what's it worth?" Reverse DCF flips it: "at today's price, what growth must be true?" That turns a fuzzy valuation into a single yes/no question you can judge — can this company actually grow that fast?
Reading it: if the implied rate is below what the business has historically delivered, the market may be too pessimistic (potential upside). If it's above what's realistic, the price needs heroics to justify. Roughly: <5% = low bar, 5–15% = reasonable, 15–25% = demanding, >25% = very aggressive. 🧮 More Valuation Methods — DDM · Multiples · Graham
💡 Four ways to value a stock — which to trust for which business ▶
Why more than one method?
No single model fits every company. A cash-rich tech firm, a dividend utility, and an asset-heavy bank each reveal their value through a different lens. Running several and seeing where they agree (or clash) is how you triangulate a sensible range instead of trusting one number.
The four lenses
  • DCF (above) — present value of future free cash flow. Best for profitable, predictable cash generators. Struggles with hypergrowth and financials.
  • DDM — values the stream of dividends. Best for stable, reliable dividend payers: utilities, consumer staples, REITs, telecoms. Useless for non-payers.
  • Multiples — what the stock is worth at a "fair" P/E (or other ratio). Best for companies with profitable peers to compare against; fast reality-check on any profitable firm.
  • Graham Number — a conservative floor from earnings & book value. Best for established value stocks with real assets and steady profits. Built for old-economy businesses, not asset-light tech.
How to use this panel
Type a ticker and hit Value It. Each method auto-fills what it can (some from the data already on this page) and leaves the rest editable. The Summary at the bottom lines up every method's fair value against the current price so you can see the spread at a glance. Each method has its own ▶ help box explaining its inputs and when to believe it.
Tip: when methods cluster tightly, you can be more confident. When they're scattered, the business is hard to value — treat any single number with caution.
💵 Dividend Discount Model how & when ▶
About DDM▶
What it does
The Gordon Growth DDM values a share as next year's dividend ÷ (required return − dividend growth). It assumes dividends grow forever at a steady rate.
Best for
Mature, stable dividend payers — utilities, consumer staples, telecoms, REITs, big banks. Companies with a long, reliable payout history.
Don't use for
Non-dividend payers (most tech/growth), or firms with erratic or newly-started dividends — the answer will be meaningless or zero.
Inputs
Annual dividend/share (auto from price × yield), a growth rate (keep below your discount rate, e.g. 2–6%), and a required return (your target, e.g. 8–10%). Growth must be < required return or the math breaks.

📊 Relative Multiples how & when ▶
About Multiples▶
What it does
Values the stock by applying a "fair" multiple to its earnings: fair price = target P/E × EPS. You can also sanity-check against P/B and P/S.
Best for
Any profitable company that has comparable peers or a stable own-history multiple. The quickest reality-check in valuation, and how most analysts actually anchor a price.
Don't use for
Unprofitable firms (no meaningful P/E), or one-off distorted earnings years. Garbage EPS in → garbage out.
Inputs
EPS (auto = price ÷ P/E) and a target P/E you consider fair — its historical average, or a peer's. Implied price = EPS × target P/E.

🏛 Graham Number how & when ▶
About the Graham Number▶
What it does
Benjamin Graham's conservative fair-value formula: √(22.5 × EPS × book value/share). The 22.5 reflects his rule that P/E × P/B shouldn't exceed 22.5 (15 × 1.5).
Best for
Established value stocks with real earnings and tangible assets — industrials, financials, mature manufacturers. A quick "floor" value for defensive investing.
Don't use for
Asset-light or high-growth companies (tech, software) — it will wildly understate them. Also meaningless if EPS or book value is negative.
Inputs
EPS (auto = price ÷ P/E) and book value/share (auto = price ÷ P/B). Both must be positive.

🎯 Valuation Summary how to read ▶
Reading the summary▶
Each method's fair value is listed with its upside/downside vs. the current price, and plotted on the line below (the vertical black line is today's price). Tight cluster = methods agree, higher confidence. Wide scatter = the business is hard to value; lean on the method that fits it best (see each card's help). Methods that don't apply (e.g. DDM for a non-payer) are shown as n/a and excluded from the average.
